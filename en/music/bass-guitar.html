The bass guitar, also called the electric bass or simply the bass, is a plucked string instrument that resembles an electric guitar but has a longer neck and lower-pitched strings.[1] It supplies the lowest notes of a band, linking the harmony of the chords to the rhythm of the drums.

History

Early electric basses appeared in the 1930s, but the first commercially successful model was the Fender Precision Bass, introduced in 1951.[2] The instrument was easier to carry and to amplify than the upright double bass, and it used frets, which made accurate tuning simpler for guitarists. These advantages led to its wide adoption in popular music during the following decades.

Construction

A bass guitar consists of a body, a neck with a fingerboard, tuning machines and magnetic pickups that convert string vibration into an electrical signal. The signal is sent to an amplifier. The scale length, the vibrating length of the strings between the nut and the bridge, is typically 34 inches (864 mm) on a standard bass.[3] Models with five or six strings add a lower string, a higher string, or both, and fretless versions omit the frets entirely.

Tuning and range

The standard four-string bass is tuned E1, A1, D2 and G2, which is one octave below the four lowest strings of a guitar.[1] Five-string models usually add a low B string, extending the range downwards.

Playing techniques

  • Fingerstyle: the strings are plucked with the index and middle fingers, producing a warm and even tone.
  • Plectrum: the strings are struck with a plectrum, a small flat tool also called a pick, which gives a brighter and more defined attack.
  • Slap and pop: the thumb strikes the string and the index finger pulls it, a percussive style common in funk.
  • Muting: the hands dampen unwanted vibrations to keep each note short and clear.

Role in ensembles

In most genres the bass and the drums form the rhythm section. The bass usually outlines the root of each chord while locking its timing to the bass drum, which gives the music a stable foundation on which melodies and chords can be built.

Learning and practice

A small set of exercises yields most of the progress for a beginner:

  1. Major and minor scales and arpeggios, to learn the fingerboard.
  2. Practice with a metronome, to develop steady timing.
  3. Simple root-and-fifth patterns, to support common chord progressions.
  4. Playing along with drum tracks, to practise locking in with the rhythm.
