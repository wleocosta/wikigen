Fixed income

Fixed income is a group of investments in which the rules for remuneration are defined at the time of purchase or are linked to a published index.[1] The investor lends money to an issuer, such as a government or a bank, and receives interest over time. The maturity date is the day on which the issuer repays the principal and the final interest.

This article is educational and does not constitute investment advice. The numbers used are hypothetical.

Types by remuneration

Fixed income securities are usually grouped by how their return is determined.[2]

Examples in Brazil

  • Tesouro Direto: a program through which the national treasury sells government bonds to individual investors.[3]
  • CDB (Certificado de Depósito Bancário): a debt security issued by a bank to raise funds, with remuneration agreed at purchase.

Income tax and fees apply to many of these securities, and the rules depend on the instrument and on the holding period. They can change, so the official sources should be consulted before any decision.

Compound interest

With compound interest, each period's interest is added to the balance, so the next period's interest is calculated on a larger amount.[4] The principal is the original amount invested, on which interest is calculated. The final value is given by:

FV = PV × (1 + i)n

where FV is the future value, PV the present value (the principal), i the interest rate per period and n the number of periods.

Hypothetical example: R$ 10,000.00 at 10% per year for three years

The rate is hypothetical, is not a forecast or a current market rate, and the example ignores taxes and fees. With simple interest, the same principal would reach R$ 13,000.00, so compounding adds R$ 310.00 in this case.

Risks

  • Credit risk: the possibility that the issuer fails to pay.
  • Interest rate risk: the price of a prefixed security can fall if market rates rise and it is sold before maturity.
  • Inflation risk: the possibility that inflation reduces the real value of the return.
  • Liquidity risk: the possibility that an investment cannot be sold quickly without a loss in value.

Accounting treatment

Accounting recognises the interest on such investments as income over the period in which it is earned, a principle known as accrual accounting. This is one example of how financial statements connect to investment analysis.
