Electronics is the field of science and engineering that deals with circuits in which the flow of electric current is controlled in order to process signals or information.[1] In this encyclopedia, the subject refers to hands-on electronics: the building and repair of circuits from basic components, often combined with a microcontroller that runs a program. This combination links software to machines that sense and act, as in automation.

Basic quantities

Three quantities describe most simple circuits.

Basic electrical quantities
QuantitySymbolUnitMeaning
VoltageVvolt (V)The electrical pressure that drives current through a circuit
CurrentIampere (A)The flow of electric charge
ResistanceRohm (Ω)The opposition to the flow of current

Ohm's law relates the three: the voltage across a resistor equals the current through it multiplied by its resistance, or V = I × R.[2] For example, to run a red LED at 20 mA from a 5 V supply, with the LED dropping about 2 V, the resistor must drop the remaining 3 V, so R = 3 V / 0.02 A = 150 Ω.

Tools and safety

A basic workbench needs a multimeter, which measures voltage, current and resistance; a breadboard, on which circuits are assembled without soldering; a soldering iron; and wire strippers. Safe practice includes:

  • Low voltage first: batteries and 5 V USB supplies are the safe place to start.
  • No mains electricity: household voltage can cause serious injury or death and should be handled only by qualified people.
  • Power off: disconnect the power before changing a circuit.
  • Check polarity: components such as LEDs, diodes and electrolytic capacitors work in one direction only, and a reversed connection can damage them.
  • Heat: a soldering iron is hot enough to cause burns, so it is kept in its stand when not in use.

This article is educational and is not a substitute for professional training.

Study approach

Theory is consolidated by building, so a practical sequence is:

  1. Learn voltage, current, resistance and Ohm's law.
  2. Build a simple circuit on a breadboard, such as an LED with a resistor.
  3. Measure the circuit with a multimeter and compare the result with the calculation.
  4. Add a microcontroller and a program that reads a sensor or switches an output.

These basic skills also allow simple low-voltage faults, such as a loose connection or a failed fuse, to be found and repaired without hiring third-party services.
